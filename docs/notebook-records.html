Records and signing

Screenshots and recordings show the direct edition. Compare editions.

A notebook page is a record as well as a document. It carries a status, tags, comments, links to the sequences it describes, and a local revision history. Signing freezes one revision and locks the page, which is tamper-evident local attestation on a single machine.

Notebook covers writing: the editor, the slash menu, tables, images, and the tree. This page covers what happens to that writing afterwards, and what a signature on it establishes.

What a page carries

Notebooks are organised as projects, notebooks, and pages. A page is a standalone rich document, stored in the same on-device workspace as your conversations and sequence blocks, so it is backed up and exported with them.

  • Rich content. Headings, lists, checklists, quotes, code blocks, tables, imported spreadsheets, and inline images.
  • A slash menu. Type / to insert a block at the caret, and filter the list by typing after the slash.
  • An @ picker. Type @ at a word boundary to search for a link target and insert it as an inline chip.
  • Tags. Freeform labels on a page, used for triage and matched by notebook search.
  • Status. Draft, In review, or Complete.
  • Comments. Threads anchored to a span of text, which can be resolved and reopened.

Set a page status

Each page carries one of three states: Draft, In review, and Complete. Select the status pill under the page title to change it. A page with no status set yet renders a quiet Draft pill.

A status change is written to the page's audit trail as a status event carrying the new value, so a page that moved to Complete has a dated entry saying so. Status is metadata rather than content: it stays editable after a page is signed, and a signature does not cover it. See The trust boundary.

Link to a sequence or a base range

A link connects a page to the work it describes. One link points at exactly one target, and the target can be any of four things:

  • An entry, which is a conversation in the workspace.
  • A block inside an entry, such as one DNA sequence.
  • An exact base range inside a block, so a note about a mutation points at the bases it describes.
  • Another notebook page.

To link a range, select it in the block first, then open the @ picker. The selected range appears as an option you can pick directly. The link stores the block and the range bounds, so it keeps pointing at those bases rather than at a label.

Read a link from either end

Links are stored once and read from both sides, so the two directions cannot drift apart.

  • On the page, the linked-entries panel lists every target the page points at. An inline chip navigates to its target when you click it, and the chip's label is derived from the live target, so renaming the target updates the chip.
  • On the entry, the workspace inspector's Overview tab shows Notebook references: every notebook page that links to this entry or to a block inside it. It appears only when at least one page references the entry.

Deleting an entry, a block, or a page removes the links that targeted it, so neither side keeps a stale reference.

Capture a revision

A revision is a snapshot of one page at one moment. It stores the title, the format, and the content as they were, plus the SHA-256 hash of those canonical bytes. Three actions create one:

  • Save version. The explicit control in the page footer. It flushes the editor's pending buffer first, so the revision holds the keystroke you just typed rather than the last render.
  • An autosnapshot. Captured in the background after a pause in editing, one per page, and skipped for a page with no meaningful content and for a locked page.
  • Signing. A signature always captures the revision it signs, so the signed bytes exist as a record in their own right.

Select History on a page to read its signatures, its revisions, and the audit events that concern it. Each row shows the actor, the time, why the revision was captured, and the short form of its content hash.

Routine background history is bounded so that autosave cannot crowd out deliberate evidence. The newest 50 autosnapshots per page are kept. Saved versions, signing revisions, and any revision a signature references are retained independently of that limit.

Recover with a restore point

A restore point is a snapshot of the whole notebook workspace, captured immediately before a destructive action. SEAL takes one before you delete a page and before you delete a notebook, and shows an Undo control afterwards.

  • Restore points are held in their own durable setting, separate from the live workspace, so one survives a crash or a restart.
  • The newest 15 are kept.
  • Reverting to a restore point overwrites the live workspace, so SEAL captures the current state as a restore point first. The revert is itself undoable.
  • A restore point carries workspace content and no matching evidence bundle, so a locked page stays locked through a restore. A restore is not a route around a signature.

If the stored notebook data cannot be read, or was written by a newer build, SEAL pauses saving rather than overwrite it, and the Notebook view shows a verification state until the workspace and its evidence pass as one bundle.

Sign and lock a revision

Signing is the point where a page stops being a working document and becomes a fixed record. Select Sign in the page footer. The dialog asks for two things: the meaning you are attesting, and your name typed twice, the second time matching the first exactly. The four meanings are Authored, Reviewed, Approved, and Witnessed.

On confirmation SEAL does four things in one transaction:

  1. Captures a revision of the page's exact current content and hashes it.
  2. Records a signature holding the entered signer name, the meaning, the time, and that content hash.
  3. Appends a signed event to the local hash-chained audit trail.
  4. Locks the page.

A locked page shows a lock line naming the meaning, the signer, and the date. Its title and content become read-only, and it cannot be deleted. Status and tags remain editable. Unlocking is a deliberate two-step control, and the unlock is written to the audit trail as its own event, so a page that was reopened for edits says so in its history.

The signer name is whatever you type into the dialog, the time comes from your computer's clock, and the evidence lives only on this machine. Read the next section before you rely on a signature for anything beyond your own bench record.

The trust boundary

The accurate description of a SEAL signature is tamper-evident local attestation. Every word of that is load-bearing, so here is what each one means in practice.

The signer name is a local assertion

The name recorded in a signature is the text entered in the dialog at signing time. SEAL captures it and pins it to the signed bytes, which is why a later change to the operator identity cannot rewrite an existing signature. It is an assertion by whoever was at the keyboard. There is no certificate authority, organisation directory, or account behind it, and the confirm-your-name step makes the action deliberate rather than authenticated.

The time is the local system clock

A signature's timestamp is read from the machine it was made on. It is as accurate as that clock, and no outside service attests to it.

There is no external trust anchor

The audit trail detects change rather than preventing it. Each event stores the previous event's hash inside its own hashed payload, so editing a past event in place, or reordering events, breaks every hash after it and the integrity check reports the break. That holds against an accident or an ordinary edit. It does not hold against someone with direct access to the database file, who could rewrite the content and recompute the whole local chain, because nothing outside the machine has ever seen the original hashes.

A signature covers the bytes, not the metadata

The hash a signature pins is taken over the page's title, format, and content. Status, tags, comments, and links sit outside it. A page can move from In review to Complete after it is signed, and the signature is unaffected, because it never described the status in the first place.

What this gives you is durable provenance for your own bench record on one machine, with a verifiable answer to the question of whether a page changed after you froze it. Anything wider, such as an externally anchored identity, an independently attested time, or evidence that survives an untrusted machine, is a different system with its own legal, procedural, and validation requirements.

Verify the audit trail

Select History on any page, then Verify integrity. SEAL re-walks the entire hash chain and reports either that the audit trail is intact, with the number of events, or the sequence number of the first event where the chain breaks.

Each event records a monotonic sequence number, a timestamp, the actor, the action, the page it concerns, a short summary, the previous event's hash, and its own SHA-256 over that canonical payload. The actions recorded are:

Keep an independent backup

SEAL's release gates test supported migrations, backup integrity, restore round trips, and verification of the local audit chain. The goal those tests support is preventing silent data loss in covered scenarios, meaning a failure that takes your data without telling you. Hardware failure, disk exhaustion, malware, losing the account or the machine, and deleting every copy all sit outside that.

Keep a backup outside the application data directory, and confirm that it restores before you rely on SEAL for work you cannot repeat. Settings and backup covers portable workspace backups, and Privacy lists where the database file lives on disk.
